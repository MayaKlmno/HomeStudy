# How adults learn Japanese, and how this course follows it

## What the research and the standard curricula say

1. **Kana first: hiragana, then katakana, then kanji learned inside words.** Mainstream
   university textbooks teach hiragana in the first lesson and katakana in the second, then add
   kanji gradually in the context of vocabulary (Genki: hiragana in L1, katakana in L2, kanji from L3,
   https://genki3.japantimes.co.jp/en/intro/ ; https://sethclydesdale.github.io/genki-study-resources/help/japanese-guide/).
   Hiragana comes first because beginners use it the most. Katakana is quick to learn once you know
   the hiragana sounds.
2. **A fixed grammar order from N5 to N4.** Genki and Minna no Nihongo follow much the same order,
   and JLPT N5/N4 lists map onto it: X は Y です → question か, の, こ/そ/あ/ど → ます verbs with を/に/で/へ/と,
   あります/います → past tense and adjectives → the て-form (てください, ている, てもいい, てはいけない) →
   the plain form (ない, た, と思う, noun-modifying clauses) → comparisons, つもり, たことがある, んです →
   N4: potential, そうだ, てみる, あげる/くれる/もらう, volitional, ておく, conditionals (たら/ば/と),
   passive, causative, honorific and humble speech
   (https://jlptsensei.com/jlpt-n5-grammar-list/ ; https://jlptsensei.com/jlpt-n4-grammar-list/ ;
   https://migaku.com/blog/japanese/japanese-grammar-n5-complete-jlpt-guide).
   The polite です/ます form comes first because adults need it with strangers. The plain form follows,
   because N4 grammar is built on it.
3. **High-frequency words and kanji first.** Kanji frequency is very skewed. The top 500 kanji
   cover about 80% of kanji in newspapers, and the top 1,000 cover about 95%
   (https://scriptin.github.io/kanji-frequency/ ; https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03200819.pdf).
   Early vocabulary should be practical and everyday: people, places, time, food, daily verbs.
4. **Retrieval practice, spaced out.** Testing yourself beats re-reading. In Karpicke & Roediger’s
   foreign-vocabulary study, repeated testing produced large gains in delayed recall and repeated
   study produced none. Spacing the retrievals out helps long-term retention
   (https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1152408 ;
   https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JEPLMC.pdf ;
   https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/modl.12879).
5. **Comprehensible input.** Learners acquire language by understanding real messages pitched
   slightly above their level (Krashen, *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*,
   https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf). Graded reading of
   authentic text, with glosses, supports this.

## How this curriculum applies it

- **Units 1–2: kana only.** Unit 1 teaches the 46 hiragana row by row (plus ゛/゜ and small ゃ as
  rules), and Unit 2 the 46 katakana through loanwords (コーヒー, ラーメン, トイレ). Each level has 4–6
  signs. Its sentences use only kana already taught (or derived by the ゛/゜ rule), with romaji, and
  are spaced like a children’s book.
- **Units 3–4: polite-form basics (N5).** は/です, か, の, これ/この/ここ, じゃありません, も, numbers and
  prices, then ます/ません, を, に/へ, で, と, time words, ませんか/ましょう, あります/います, position words.
- **Units 5–6: the rest of N5.** The past, い-/な-adjectives, 好き/上手, counters, どう/どんな; then the
  て-form family (てください, ている, てもいい, てはいけない, linking), たい and stem + に行く.
- **Unit 7: the plain form.** だ, dictionary form, ない, た/なかった, と思う, と言う, relative clauses,
  ないでください, なければならない. It is anchored to 走れメロス, which is narrated in the plain form.
- **Units 8–10: N4.** より/一番, つもり, なる, たり, たことがある, んです, すぎる, 方がいい/ので; the
  potential, そう (looks), てみる, 欲しい, かもしれない, giving and receiving, the volitional, ておく, たら;
  hearsay そうだ, ば, と, ながら, てしまう, passive, causative, honorific and humble verbs.
- **Vocabulary.** Everyday, high-frequency words (family, school and work, food, time, weather,
  travel, shopping, health). Kanji appear only inside words, always with kana and romaji.
- **Retrieval and spacing.** Each level drills its new items (recognition → recall → typing). The
  app’s spaced review brings earlier words back later, so each review is an effortful retrieval.
- **Comprehensible input.** Every level ends with one real, glossed and translated line from
  literature, and each unit closes with a longer reading and questions. The books go from easiest
  to hardest: Niimi Nankichi’s children’s stories, then Miyazawa Kenji and Akutagawa, then Dazai,
  then Sōseki.

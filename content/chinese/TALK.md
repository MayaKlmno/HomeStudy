# Talk mode (Mandarin): how it teaches speaking, and the 80 dialogs

## What the research says, and what the dialogs do about it
1. **Retrieval, spaced out.** Pimsleur’s *A Memory Schedule* (1967) recalls a new item after longer and longer gaps: 5 s, 25 s, 2 min, 10 min, 1 h, 5 h, 1 day, 5 days, 25 days…
   ([ERIC ED012150](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED012150)). Testing yourself beats re-reading (Roediger & Karpicke 2006), and spread-out practice beats cramming (Cepeda et al. 2006).
   → Each line is heard twice and repeated (seconds), then recalled from an English cue in the dialog (minutes), and its chunks come back in later levels (days).
2. **Output: say it, don’t just hear it.** Swain’s output hypothesis: speaking makes learners notice the gap between what they want to say and what they can say, and lets them test their guesses
   ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)). → Every graded turn is spoken from memory. From level 11 the cue gives a goal (“Ask how much the bananas are”), not a translation, so the learner builds the sentence.
3. **Tones in whole phrases.** L2 learners pick out tones well on single syllables but less well in two-syllable words and sentences, where the neighbouring tones change them
   ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2021.689423/full); [Speech Communication 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167639317301425)).
   → Lines are short, whole phrases said at normal speed and then slowly. Pinyin marks the 不/一 changes (bú shì, yí gè), and glosses point out 3 + 3 (nǐ hǎo → ní hǎo).
4. **High-frequency chunks first.** Set phrases people use every day (不客气, 哪里哪里, 别提了, 慢慢来, 麻烦…, 师傅, 老板) are learned whole and reused.
5. **HSK 1 → 4 spoken grammar, in order** ([AllSet grammar by level](https://resources.allsetlearning.com/chinese/grammar/Grammar_points_by_level)):
   是 / 很 / 吗 / 呢 → 要 / 想 and measure words (杯 碗 瓶 张 双 条 辆) → 多少钱 / 两 / 比 / 最 → 在 + place, 往 / 离 / 从…到 → 了, 能 / 会 / 可以, verb + 不了 →
   得 (degree), 过, 还没 → 打算, 先…然后, 因为, 要不 → 是…的, 把, 越…越, 要是 → (41–80) 被, 连…都, 不管…都, 一边…一边, 既然/因为…所以, 差点儿, 只好, resultative and directional endings (修好, 写下来, 坐不下, 说不出来), 要不然, 等…以后.
6. **Fillers and hedges are learned on purpose.** Spoken Mandarin is full of discourse markers that soften and organise a turn; corpus work shows they carry attitude and hold the floor
   ([ni zhidao study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8687356/)). → Levels 41–80 teach 那个…, 其实, 说实话, 我觉得吧, 不过, 反而, 另外, 总的来说, 看情况 as whole chunks.
7. **Disagreeing politely is a skill, not a word.** The polite pattern is: grant the other view, soften with 不过, then say your own — 你说得有道理 / 话是这么说 / 这倒是 / 我明白你的意思 / 我不太同意
   ([overview](https://lcchineseschool.com/the-art-of-disagreement-expressing-opinions-politely-in-chinese/)). Levels 56–60 drill exactly that shape.
8. **Phone Chinese has its own openings.** 喂, 您好；请问您是哪位；我是…；您现在方便吗；麻烦您转告；那就先这样 ([phone etiquette](https://preply.com/en/blog/phone-calls-in-chinese/)). Levels 42 and 46–50 use nothing else.
9. **Listening stretches ahead.** The other speaker’s lines are a little harder than yours (comprehensible input), and for levels 1–15 the narrator translates them.

## Length and recycling
Your lines grow from about 2 tiles (≈4 characters: 你好, 我很好) to about 14 tiles (≈28 characters) at level 80, checked by `tools/difficulty.js`.
Each level adds a few new phrases. The rest reuse what came before (多少钱, 在哪儿, 可以…吗, 帮我…, 太…了, 有点儿, 麻烦你, 没关系), usually inside a longer sentence. Numbers are written in characters, and `alt` accepts digits and the southern forms (这里 / 哪里 / 一点).
The learner is always 马克 (Mark): English, living in London, married, one older sister in Australia, an engineer at a car company. The same people come back — 王丽 the neighbour, 张明, 李老师, Anna, and from level 41 his Beijing colleagues 小刘 and 陈经理.

## The 16 themes
1. **Greetings and introductions** (1–5): hello, names, how are you, where you’re from, introducing a friend.
2. **Café and restaurant** (6–10): ordering coffee, tea with a friend, a bowl of noodles, who pays, street food.
3. **Numbers, prices and shopping** (11–15): fruit by the jin, bargaining, trying on shoes, the supermarket, choosing a present (比 / 最).
4. **Directions and transport** (16–20): asking the way, subway tickets, a taxi, the right bus, lost and on the phone.
5. **Hotel, travel and problems** (21–25): checking in, a broken air conditioner, a train ticket, a lost phone, checking out.
6. **Small talk** (26–30): family, work, hobbies, the weather, making a new friend (了 / 过 / 得).
7. **Plans, invitations and time** (31–35): inviting a friend out, holiday plans, turning down an invitation, running late, booking a table.
8. **Past, health, opinions and a story** (36–40): the doctor, “have you ever…?”, giving an opinion, a terrible morning, and the story of your trip to China.
9. **Work and study** (41–45): meeting a colleague, a phone call about a meeting, reporting in the meeting, asking for instructions, owning up to a mistake.
10. **On the phone and online** (46–50): customer service, leaving a message, a parcel that never came, booking an appointment, a bad signal.
11. **Telling stories** (51–55): yesterday’s queue, a surprise at the door, a near miss, the train you missed, passing on what someone told you.
12. **Opinions and disagreeing politely** (56–60): a film, agreeing warmly, disagreeing without rudeness, giving reasons, changing your mind.
13. **Feelings and helping people** (61–65): worry, relief, congratulations and sympathy, comforting a friend, asking a favour.
14. **Everyday admin and problems** (66–70): the bank, a form at the post office, a complaint, cancelling a booking, clearing up a misunderstanding.
15. **Culture, food and life here** (71–75): explaining British habits, asking about wedding customs, how a dish is made, Spring Festival, what surprised you.
16. **Longer conversation** (76–80): plans and dreams, a job interview, catching up with an old friend, a three-way decision, and how you learned Chinese.

# Spanish Talk mode — research and outline

80 hands-free spoken dialogs (`js/tracks/spanish/talk.js`, `lang: 'es-ES'`). The aim is to hold a real conversation. Levels 1–40 get you through the survival situations (A1–A2); levels 41–80 take you from handling transactions to holding a conversation (A2→B1): longer turns, opinions, narration and the unexpected.

## What the research says about learning to speak
- **Retrieval beats re-listening.** Trying to recall a phrase makes it stick far better than hearing it again (the testing effect: Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). So every `you` turn gives an English goal and the learner has to *produce* the Spanish before hearing it.
- **Anticipation and graduated-interval recall (Pimsleur, 1967).** Prompt, pause for the learner to answer, then confirm. Bring items back at growing gaps. Here each level recycles earlier chunks (*por favor*, *muchas gracias*, *¿cuánto cuesta…?*, *a las…*, *perdone*, *quería…*, *me encanta*, *no pasa nada*, *menos mal*, *me viene bien*). A few whole lines come back exactly two levels apart, e.g. *Encantado* (2, 3), *Un café con leche* (6, 10), *¿Puedo pagar con tarjeta?* (9, 14); *Muchas gracias, muy amable* returns at 16, 20 and then **66**, fifty levels later, and level 80 replays the very first café lines inside a long sentence.
- **Output hypothesis (Swain, 1985).** Speaking pushes learners to notice what they can't say yet. The cues move from literal translations (levels 1–10) to goals (level 11 on), and from level 41 the goal is a whole speech act — *concede the point, then give your reason*, *report what she told you*, *take some of the blame*.
- **Chunks first.** Adults reach fluency faster with memorised whole phrases (*¿Me da…?*, *Me queda grande*, *No pasa nada*, *Es que…*, *No te vas a creer…*, *Cuánto lo siento*) than with grammar rules alone (Nattinger & DeCarrico; Lewis's lexical approach).
- **Discourse markers and hedges are what B1 sounds like.** Spoken-corpus work on Spanish marcadores (*bueno, pues, o sea, es que, la verdad es que, hombre, venga, vale, ya, en parte sí, a lo mejor, seguramente, depende*) shows they are among the most frequent items in conversation and carry the politeness. Levels 41–80 teach them on purpose: *pues* and *la verdad es que* (41), *es que* (45), *o sea* (50, 73), *la verdad* (53), *en parte sí* and *puede que me equivoque* (58), *depende* (59), *hombre* and *venga* (60), *pues nada* (74).
- **High-frequency spoken words.** Most lines use the top ~1,000 words of spoken Spanish (Davies, *A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*; CREA oral corpus): ser/estar/tener/ir/querer/poder/parecer/quedar, the numbers, times and days.
- **CEFR / Plan Curricular del Instituto Cervantes.** A1–A2 covers predictable everyday exchanges. B1 adds narrating experiences, expressing and justifying an opinion, *posicionarse a favor o en contra* politely, transactional phone calls, complaints, and coping when things go wrong — which is exactly the arc of themes 9–16.
- **Phone Spanish is its own skill.** Ringing, being screened and being cut off need set phrases that never come up face to face: *¿Dígame?* (*¿aló?*, *¿bueno?* in Latin America), *¿De parte de quién?*, *No cuelgue*, *está reunido*, *le paso*, *¿Le puedo dejar un mensaje?*, *se corta*, *te oigo fatal*, *solo he oído la mitad*. Levels 42 and 46–50 are built on them.
- **Register.** *Usted* with strangers, staff and older people; *tú* with friends and colleagues. Spain's form goes in `t`, and the Latin American form goes in `alt` and the gloss: coche/carro/auto, billete/boleto, entradas/boletos, patatas/papas, móvil/celular, ordenador/computadora, tarta/pastel, cola/fila, vale/está bien, enfadarse/enojarse, coger/agarrar, merece la pena/vale la pena, vosotros/ustedes (32, 72, 74), *he entendido*/*entendí*.
- **The learner is a man.** Alex Smith, 34, from London. Where a line carries a masculine ending — *encantado, preocupado, tranquilo, seguro, soltero, solo* — the feminine form is in `alt` and named in the gloss, so a woman can answer naturally.
- **Length ramp.** The learner's lines average about 2 words at level 1 and about 16 at level 80, climbing in a straight line and never backsliding, checked by `tools/difficulty.js`. From level 41 a turn is no longer one clause: it carries a reason, a detail or a hedge as well (*…, porque lo tengo todo cerca*, *…, si te digo la verdad*, *…, y ahora te digo por qué*). That is what takes the learner from answering questions to holding up their end of a conversation. Listening (`them` lines) stays a little ahead of speaking, and the `alt` forms are full-length paraphrases, not short cuts, so speech recognition can't be passed by saying almost nothing.

## Outline (16 themes × 5 levels)
1. **Greetings & introductions (1–5):** good morning/afternoon, thanks, bye; name and origin with usted, then tú; "I don't understand / slower, please"; first day at school.
2. **Café & restaurant (6–10):** ordering a coffee, a table for two, ordering lunch, the bill and paying by card, coffee with a friend (tú, "I'll pay!").
3. **Numbers, prices & shopping (11–15):** market by the kilo, trying on clothes and sizes, age and phone number at a gym, the supermarket till, a gift-wrapped present.
4. **Directions & transport (16–20):** where is…/is it far, train tickets, taxi, bus stops, the metro to the Prado.
5. **Hotel, travel & problems (21–25):** checking in, something broken in the room, lost luggage, changing a booking, a mistake on the bill.
6. **Small talk (26–30):** where you live and your job, family, weekend hobbies, the weather, a language-exchange chat.
7. **Plans, invitations & time (31–35):** inviting and arranging a time, saying no politely, booking a table by phone, weekend plans with *ir a*, running late.
8. **Past, health & opinions (36–40):** a holiday (preterite), at the doctor's, opinions about a film, a terrible day, how you met Javi (preterite + imperfect).
9. **Work and study (41–45):** your job and your classes, a phone call to a colleague, a short meeting, asking for and giving instructions, apologising for a mistake at work.
10. **On the phone and online (46–50):** phoning a company, being put on hold and leaving a message, a delivery that never arrived, an appointment booked by phone, a line that keeps cutting out.
11. **Telling stories (51–55):** what you did yesterday, a story with a surprise, a near miss on the bike, a trip that went wrong, passing on what someone else told you (reported speech).
12. **Opinions and disagreeing politely (56–60):** a book and its film, agreeing strongly, disagreeing without being rude, giving reasons and *depende*, changing your mind.
13. **Feelings and helping people (61–65):** worry, relief, congratulations and sympathy in one conversation, comforting a friend, asking for and offering a favour.
14. **Everyday admin and problems (66–70):** a blocked card at the bank, a form and its questions, a complaint that gets resolved, cancelling a membership, clearing up a misunderstanding.
15. **Culture, food and life here (71–75):** explaining Christmas at home, asking about meal times, kisses and tipping, how a tortilla is made, a village fiesta, what surprised you here.
16. **Longer conversation (76–80):** plans and dreams, viewing a flat, catching up with an old friend, a three-way discussion with Javi and Carmen, and finally the story of how you learned Spanish — the longest turns in the course.

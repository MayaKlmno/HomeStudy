# French Talk mode — how it teaches speaking, and the 80-level outline

## What the research says
1. **Recall out loud beats listening again.** Retrieving an answer from memory strengthens it far more
   than restudying ([Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/)). Pimsleur’s
   *anticipation* principle applies this to speech: an English cue, a pause to produce the line, then the
   model answer ([Pimsleur method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pimsleur_Language_Programs)).
2. **Graduated-interval recall.** Items come back at growing gaps (Pimsleur 1967: 5 s, 25 s, 2 min,
   10 min, 1 h…), a form of the spacing effect ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)).
   Here: hear it in *Learn*, recall it minutes later in the *Dialog*, meet it again in later levels.
3. **Output drives accuracy.** Swain’s output hypothesis: having to speak shows learners the gap between
   what they want to say and what they can say ([comprehensible output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)).
4. **Chunks first.** Fluent speech leans on ready-made phrases (*je voudrais…, c’est combien?, ça marche*);
   learning them whole gives beginners usable speech long before the grammar behind them.
5. **CEFR spoken interaction, A1 → B1.** A1: simple questions and answers on immediate needs, with a
   patient partner. A2: short routine exchanges (shopping, transport, plans). B1: handle less routine
   situations, explain a problem, give opinions, tell what happened
   ([CEFR self-assessment grid](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages)).

## How the levels apply it
- Your lines grow along one straight line, from ~2 words (level 1) to ~16 (level 80), and never dip
  back: by level 41 a turn is a full sentence with a reason or a detail attached.
- Cues are literal in levels 1–10 (“Say: …”), then goals (“Ask how much the tomatoes are”).
- Each level adds about three new phrases and recycles the rest; a line appears in at most three levels.
- `alt` answers are as long as the line itself, so speech recognition can’t be passed with a couple of words.
- Everyday spoken register: *vous* with strangers; *tu* arrives with a friend of a friend (level 5) and is
  negotiated explicitly (*On peut se tutoyer?*, level 28). Dropped *ne* and short forms go in `alt`.
- Survival repair phrases come early: *Pardon? Je ne comprends pas. Plus lentement. Vous parlez anglais?*
- From level 41 the learner also needs the words that hold a turn together, so every theme drills the
  spoken markers and hedges: *franchement, en fait, du coup, bon, alors, ça dépend, par contre, écoutez,
  je dirais, à mon avis*. They are what turns correct sentences into conversation.
- One cast throughout: Alex (the learner — in IT, two children, books under the name Martin), the friends
  Tom, Léa, Marc and Claire, and at work Nadia and Madame Dubois.

## Outline (16 themes × 5 levels)

**A1–A2, levels 1–40 — surviving**
1. **Greetings & introductions** (1–5): bonjour/merci/au revoir, names, “I don’t understand”, first *tu*.
2. **Café & restaurant** (6–10): order a coffee, the bill, a table for two, a meal, dessert.
3. **Numbers, prices & shopping** (11–15): market, clothes sizes, paying, tickets and opening days, a refund.
4. **Directions & transport** (16–20): asking the way, metro, taxi, train tickets, a late train.
5. **Hotel, travel & problems** (21–25): check-in, a broken shower, lost luggage, car hire, a wrong bill.
6. **Small talk** (26–30): family, work, switching to *tu*, the weather, weekend and hobbies (first past tense).
7. **Plans & invitations** (31–35): accept an invitation, plan a day, running late, book by phone, reschedule.
8. **Past, health & opinions** (36–40): the doctor, a holiday, a film, an accident, and a full story.

**A2→B1, levels 41–80 — holding a conversation**
9. **Work & study** (41–45): your job and studies, a call to a colleague, a meeting, giving instructions,
   owning up to a mistake at work.
10. **On the phone & online** (46–50): phoning a company, being put on hold and leaving a message, a parcel
   that never came, an appointment booked by phone, a bad line (*j’ai compris un mot sur deux*).
11. **Telling stories** (51–55): yesterday in order, a story with a surprise, a near miss, a holiday that went
   wrong, passing on what someone else said (reported speech, imperfect).
12. **Opinions & disagreeing politely** (56–60): a book, agreeing hard, disagreeing while conceding a point,
   giving reasons, changing your mind.
13. **Feelings & helping people** (61–65): worry, relief, congratulations and sympathy, comforting a friend,
   asking a favour and offering one back.
14. **Everyday admin & problems** (66–70): the bank, a form and its questions, a complaint that gets fixed,
   cancelling a subscription, clearing up a misunderstanding.
15. **Culture, food & life here** (71–75): your own country’s habits, local customs, a recipe, the fourteenth
   of July, what surprised you in France.
16. **Longer conversation** (76–80): plans and dreams, a flat viewing, meeting an old friend after ten years,
   a three-way decision at work, and the story of learning French — the longest turns in the course.
